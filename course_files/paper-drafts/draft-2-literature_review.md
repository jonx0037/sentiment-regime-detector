# Literature Review

1. Literature Review

2.1 Transformer Models in Financial Sentiment Analysis

The evolution of financial sentiment analysis mirrors the broader transition in NLP from rule-based methods to contextual language models. Loughran and McDonald (2011) showed that generic sentiment dictionaries yield low precision for financial texts because words like "liability" carry distinct semantic valence in finance, establishing the need for domain-specific resources. Araci (2019) introduced FinBERT, a BERT model fine-tuned on financial news and reports that improved accuracy by 15% over prior methods and performed well even with as few as 500 labeled samples, demonstrating the viability of transfer learning for financial NLP. Mishev et al. (2020) systematically compared lexicons through transformers in over 100 experiments, finding that BERT and ALBERT-xxlarge achieved Matthews Correlation Coefficients of 0.895 and 0.881, respectively, while distilled models like DistilBERT retained over 95% of BERT's accuracy with 40% fewer parameters—making them suitable for production deployment.

The emergence of LLMs has expanded the toolkit for financial sentiment analysis, though with important trade-offs. Zero-shot and few-shot evaluations show that general-purpose LLMs can classify financial texts without supervised adaptation: Fatouros et al. (2023) reported approximately 35% higher sentiment classification accuracy with ChatGPT 3.5 over FinBERT for forex sentiment, while Shen and Zhang (2024) found that GPT-4o with few-shot prompting achieved competitive results, though FinBERT with domain-specific pretraining still outperformed on precision, recall, and F1-score. Fine-tuned LLMs show further gains—Konstantinidis et al. (2024) developed FinLlama using LoRA adaptation, achieving 44.7% higher cumulative returns than FinBERT-based portfolios, and Luo and Gong (2024) improved Financial PhraseBank accuracy from 0.86 to 0.90 through supervised fine-tuning of LLaMA-2.

Systematic benchmarks confirm these patterns. Nasiopoulos et al. (2025) found fine-tuned GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini achieved 87.79% accuracy, outperforming BERT models by approximately 9% but at substantially higher computational cost. Mahendran et al. (2025) noted that DistilBERT retains 97% of BERT's capabilities at half the parameters, making it suitable for real-time monitoring. Liu et al. (2024) emphasized that practical LLM deployment in finance requires careful attention to inference latency and domain-specific calibration. Ergun and Sefer (2025) proposed FinSentiment, a transfer-learning framework producing finance-specific versions of BERT, RoBERTa, GPT, Llama, and T5, finding that RoBERTa pretrained on financial text achieved state-of-the-art results even with as few as 250 labeled samples—a result particularly relevant to our ensemble design.

Corpus composition and feature engineering also influence model performance. Delgadillo et al. (2024) developed FinSoSent, pretrained on 49 million words of financial news, and found through over 860 experiments that hyperparameter configuration is as important as domain-specific pretraining, with accuracy remaining in the 50–60% range even for specialized models. Cicekyurt and Bakal (2025) showed that BERT-based knowledge transfer to stock market tweets yields significant gains over training from scratch, particularly with limited labeled data. Sun et al. (2025) introduced EnhancedFinSentiBERT, a three-branch architecture combining financial pretraining, dictionary knowledge embedding, and neutral feature extraction, achieving F1 scores of 87.0–97.6% across benchmark datasets and demonstrating that architectural innovations beyond model scaling remain valuable. Recent synthesis work by Todd et al. (2024) and Ehsan et al. (2025) identifies recurring weaknesses in financial sentiment studies, including inconsistent benchmark design and under-reporting of deployment trade-offs, while findings from Moradi-Kamali et al. (2025) and Gu and Song (2026) confirm that finance-specific language modeling improves directional signal quality, though gains are sensitive to task framing and temporal validation.

2.2 Financial Sentiment and Market Prediction

The theoretical foundations of behavioral finance trace to Keynes' (1936/1973) concept of "animal spirits," which anticipated research demonstrating that markets are arenas of collective psychology, not merely information-processing mechanisms. Baker and Wurgler (2007) operationalized investor sentiment through a composite index derived from six market-based proxies (closed-end fund discount, NYSE turnover, IPO volume, first-day IPO returns, equity share in new issues, and dividend premium). Their finding that sentiment predicts cross-sectional stock returns—with effects concentrated in hard-to-arbitrage, hard-to-value stocks—established sentiment as a systematic factor, with a 0.43 correlation with contemporaneous returns and significant predictive power during sentiment extremes.

Bollen et al. (2011) conducted a foundational study using approximately 9.8 million tweets, finding that the "Calm" dimension of aggregate mood demonstrated Granger causality with DJIA movements at lags of 2–6 days, with a Self-Organizing Fuzzy Neural Network achieving 86.7% directional accuracy—establishing that social media mood contains forward-looking market information. Renault (2017) extended this to intraday timeframes using StockTwits, finding that first-half-hour sentiment predicts last-half-hour returns (74–76% accuracy), driven primarily by novice traders whose sentiment extremes often precede market turning points.

Cryptocurrency markets—characterized by 24/7 trading, high retail participation, and strong social media presence—provide a natural environment for sentiment research. Kraaijeveld and De Smedt (2020) found significant predictive relationships between Twitter sentiment and crypto returns for Bitcoin, Bitcoin Cash, and Litecoin with 1–4 day lead times, while noting that 1–14% of tweets originated from bots. Roumeliotis et al. (2024) benchmarked language models for crypto sentiment, showing that fine-tuned GPT-4 achieved 86.7% accuracy versus FinBERT at 84.3%, demonstrating near-parity at far lower computational cost. Raheman et al. (2022) found that interpretable models often outperformed deep learning for sentiment-based crypto prediction, with peak predictive power at a 2-day lag. Trushkovskyi (2025) confirmed bidirectional Granger causality between sentiment and crypto returns, finding that sentiment signals survive transaction costs—validating their use in regime detection.

Cross-asset sentiment spillovers have been documented across market boundaries. Caferra (2022) found bidirectional sentiment transmission between Bitcoin and S&P 500 markets using Transfer Entropy methods, with entropy-based approaches outperforming traditional VAR models—demonstrating that spillovers are fundamentally nonlinear. Cao et al. (2025) showed that firms with higher sentiment connectedness face elevated crash risk, particularly during market extremes, when network-level sentiment measures outperformed individual-firm sentiment. Nyakurukwa and Seetharam (2025) mapped sentiment networks across DJIA stocks, decomposing connectedness into short-term (1–5 days), medium-term (5–20 days), and long-term (20+ days) components, finding that negative news exhibits higher connectedness than positive sentiment and that connectedness peaks during globally significant events.

This convergence across independent studies strengthens the foundation for sentiment-based regime detection. The consistent 1–6 day lead times suggest an optimal window for regime-transition early-warning systems. The data layer supporting these analyses has expanded in parallel, with Fottner et al. (2022), Dong et al. (2024), and Xu et al. (2025) proposing multimodal financial datasets that highlight a shift toward reproducible, multi-source benchmarks.

Despite encouraging evidence, Kengmegni (2024) found that agreement ratios between sentiment signals and next-day price movements hover around 0.5—essentially random chance—and that market efficiency has increased, with prediction error standard deviations declining from 0.2 in 2009 to 0.065 by 2023. Critically, economy-wide sentiment measures outperformed stock-specific sentiment, supporting our research design's focus on cross-asset aggregate sentiment rather than single-security approaches. The conclusion that "next-day stock prediction remains elusive" underscores the importance of regime-level analysis over precise return prediction.

2.3 Cross-Asset Sentiment Analysis

Integrating sentiment signals across multiple markets remains a frontier with significant implications. Building on the cross-asset spillover evidence reviewed in Section 2.2, Wang et al. (2024) found asymmetric momentum transmission between China's stock and bond markets, with hybrid funds acting as intermediaries—demonstrating that institutional investment vehicles can amplify or dampen cross-asset sentiment propagation. Yang et al. (2025) introduced a cross-asset risk management framework leveraging GPT-4 and Llama-3-30b for real-time monitoring across equities, fixed-income, and currencies, achieving 82.1% accuracy in predicting market shifts—substantially outperforming traditional methods (74.0–75.2%). These findings demonstrate the practical feasibility of unified cross-asset sentiment monitoring systems.

Foreign exchange markets present unique opportunities for sentiment analysis given their 24-hour trading and macroeconomic sensitivity. Olaiyapo (2024) combined lexicon-based analysis with Naive Bayes classification to achieve 85% accuracy for USD-related sentiment signals, generating over 12% profit when combined with technical indicators. Dakalbab et al. (2025) advanced forex prediction through cross-modal attention mechanisms integrating technical and sentiment analysis, achieving 82.9% accuracy across major currency pairs. Sibande et al. (2021) established that forex markets exhibit regime-specific sentiment effects: extreme bullish or bearish sentiment strengthened anti-herding behavior, while average sentiment produced weaker effects—suggesting that real-time sentiment monitoring can identify periods of heightened speculative activity.

The integration of sentiment into multi-asset portfolio management demonstrates practical applications. Sarfarazurrehman et al. (2025) showed that DRL and LSTM-based approaches for cross-asset risk analysis achieved cumulative returns of 29.52% with a Sharpe ratio of 0.98, outperforming traditional Mean-Variance Optimization. Pankwaen et al. (2025) developed an adaptive multi-asset framework that maintained effectiveness across major disruptions, including COVID-19 and the 2022 crypto crash, suggesting that dynamic, sentiment-aware approaches are essential for cross-asset regime detection.

Commodities exhibit unique sentiment dynamics. Shi (2025) developed a sentiment-based GARCH-MIDAS hybrid model to explain the 2020–2022 anomaly where gold prices rose 40% despite a 12% increase in the US Dollar Index. Using FinBERT-scored sentiment, the model reduced out-of-sample prediction errors by 18.7% over traditional volatility models. Negative sentiment had 1.8 times the impact on volatility as positive sentiment—consistent with loss-aversion theory—and sentiment factors accounted for approximately 15% of previously unobserved heteroskedasticity in long-term volatility components. This finding directly informs our framework's GARCH-based volatility layer.

The cross-asset evidence reviewed here—from crypto-equity spillovers to commodity safe-haven dynamics—demonstrates both the feasibility and value of unified sentiment analysis frameworks, motivating our research design that synthesizes sentiment across all four asset classes for regime detection.

2.4 Market Regime Detection

Having established that transformer-based sentiment analysis achieves strong classification performance and that sentiment signals demonstrate predictive power across asset classes, we now address integrating these insights for regime detection. Identifying market regimes—distinct periods characterized by different return distributions, volatility patterns, and investor behavior—enables dynamic allocation, downside hedging, and regime-specific opportunity capture.

Classical regime detection relies on threshold-based indicators—volatility thresholds (VIX above 30 for Risk-Off, below 15 for complacent Risk-On), moving-average crossovers, yield-curve inversion, and Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) that infer latent state transitions. The fundamental limitation is lag: these methods identify regimes after transitions have already progressed. Baker and Wurgler (2007) suggested that behavioral signals may provide earlier indicators, though their proxies relied on indirect measures rather than direct textual sentiment extraction.

While HMMs have been the standard for regime detection, Shu et al. (2024) demonstrate critical limitations: HMMs are highly sensitive to daily noise, producing "whipsaw" signals that degrade performance through excessive transaction costs. Statistical Jump Models (JMs) address this by incorporating a discrete jump penalty directly into the objective function, mathematically enforcing regime persistence and requiring substantial evidence before triggering state changes. Empirically, JMs reduce annualized portfolio turnover by approximately two-thirds versus HMMs (44% vs. 141% for S&P 500) while improving risk-adjusted returns, reducing maximum drawdown by 10–15 percentage points, and increasing compound annual growth rates by 1–4% across U.S., German, and Japanese equity indices from 1990 to 2023. This persistence-enforcing mechanism is central to our Layer 2 design.

Zhang et al. (2020) developed an explainable ML framework using hierarchical clustering for regime-based allocation, achieving 22.53% annualized returns with a Sharpe ratio of 1.06. Suárez-Cetrulo et al. (2023) reviewed 140 studies on ML for financial prediction under regime change, finding that most conventional techniques struggle with abrupt structural changes—the exact characteristic distinguishing regime transitions from normal fluctuations—and noting that the literatures on concept drift and regime switching have developed largely independently despite addressing similar challenges.

The application of sentiment to regime detection remains an emerging frontier. Bollen et al. (2011) established sentiment as a potentially leading indicator by showing that specific emotional dimensions (calm and anxiety) correlate with subsequent market movements, suggesting shifts in investor psychology precede price adjustments. Renault (2017) demonstrated that domain-specific lexicons are essential, as predictability disappeared when using standard dictionaries. Micaletti (2022) introduced relative sentiment—the gap between institutional and individual expectations—achieving 400–700 basis point annual outperformance across U.S., Europe, Japan, and Asia ex-Japan markets, with a 50-percentage-point return differential driven by sentiment state alone.

Mixed-frequency data presents additional methodological challenges. Cai et al. (2024) addressed this through an MF-EEMD-ML system integrating half-hourly sentiment with three-minute return prediction, achieving 19.18% MAE reduction over traditional approaches, and showing that sentiment impact persists across seven intraday periods. Shao et al. (2024) developed the HD-SURDLM framework integrating sentiment from 2.5 million tweets using VADER, TextBlob, and RoBERTa, but noted a critical practical trade-off: RoBERTa-based extraction increased computational costs from seconds to up to 14 hours compared with simpler methods. These temporal dynamics suggest a natural hierarchy—intraday noise signals, short-term momentum, and medium-term regime transitions—with our framework targeting the medium-term detection horizon.

2.5 Research Gaps and Hypotheses

The preceding review reveals several critical gaps. First, no framework systematically aggregates sentiment across equities, cryptocurrency, forex, and commodities to detect portfolio-level regime transitions. Second, the established 1–6 day sentiment lead time has not been exploited for regime-level early warning systems. Third, cross-asset sentiment networks remain unexplored despite evidence of individual network connectedness effects. Fourth, the practical integration of multi-source sentiment with regime detection algorithms has not been attempted.

Despite progress in both financial NLP and econometric modeling, a critical gap persists at the intersection. Konstantinidis et al. (2024) established FinLlama as a superior sentiment classifier but limited application to standard portfolio construction, neglecting structural regime identification. Shu et al. (2024) validated Statistical Jump Models but relied exclusively on endogenous price features, ignoring exogenous sentiment signals. Yang et al. (2025) applied LLMs to cross-asset monitoring, and Shi (2025) integrated GARCH-MIDAS with sentiment, but no existing research has incorporated transformer-based sentiment depth into the persistence-enforcing framework of Statistical Jump Models for a cross-asset universe. This research addresses this gap by constructing the first Two-Layer Sentiment Regime Detector, synthesizing generative AI sentiment with econometric jump penalties across Equities, Crypto, Forex, and Commodities.

Based on the literature review, we hypothesize:

H1 (Leading Indicator Hypothesis): Cross-asset sentiment aggregation acts as a leading indicator of market regime changes, preceding VIX-based regime detection by 1-5 trading days. This hypothesis is supported by findings from Bollen et al. (2011), showing a 2–6-day predictive lead time with 86.7% accuracy; Caferra (2022), illustrating sentiment-driven cross-market connections; and Trushkovskyi (2025), confirming Granger causality between sentiment and returns.

H2 (Divergence Signal Hypothesis): Sentiment divergence across asset classes (e.g., equities bullish while crypto bearish) signals an impending shift between Risk-On and Risk-Off regimes. Caferra (2022) found that sentiment connectedness effectively identifies market linkages, suggesting that disconnection or divergence may indicate regime instability. Wang et al. (2024) demonstrated asymmetric cross-asset momentum transmission supporting this mechanism.

H3 (Network Effect Hypothesis): Sentiment connectedness intensity will be associated with regime transition probability, with higher connectedness during stable regimes and rapid disconnection before transitions. Sibande et al. (2021) identified regime-specific sentiment effects in currency markets, supporting the idea of state-dependent sentiment dynamics.
